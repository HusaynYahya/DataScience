Ahkam of wealth

Khums Calculator

Khums is not a tax on what you earn. It is a fifth of what is left on the day your khums year closes. Enter what you still hold on that day, take out what is not liable, and the fifth — and the two halves it divides into — is worked out below, according to the rulings of Sayyid Ali al-Husayni al-Sistani (may Allah prolong his life).

1 Your khums year

Your khums day. For someone on a salary it is the anniversary of the first pay ever received; for a trader, of the first profit. If you have never fixed one, fix it now and keep it.

      The rulings in brief

      What khums falls on

      Seven things: the surplus of income from earnings and gains; what is extracted from a mine; a treasure-trove; lawful wealth that has become mixed with unlawful; gems brought up by diving; spoils of war; and land a dhimmi buys from a Muslim. For almost everyone it is the first that matters, and this calculator is built around it.

      The khums year

      Everyone who earns has a khums year, and it begins of itself — not by choosing a date, but by the first income. For a salaried person, the day the first pay was received; for a trader, the first profit; for a farmer, the first harvest. That day, a year on, is your khums day, and it stays yours for life. You may pay a fifth of each income as it arrives instead, and many do; then the year's end is only a reckoning of what was missed.

      What counts as an expense of the year

      Whatever was actually spent on yourself and those you support, in keeping with your standing and not in extravagance: food, rent, clothing, furnishing, travel, medicine, the hajj and ziyarah, a wedding, a dowry given, gifts, hospitality, taxes, charity, books, the repayment of debts. Two cautions follow from the word actually:

      • A budget is not an expense. Money set aside for a cost that never came is surplus, and bears khums.
      • Economy is not a loss. Living below what your standing allows leaves more over — and the more that is left, the more khums there is. That is not a penalty; it is only what the word surplus means.

      What is left over — the part people miss

      • Goods bought and not used. Provisions still in the cupboard on the khums day — rice, oil, tea — bear khums at what they are worth now, not what you paid. So do unopened purchases of any kind.
      • Goods used, even once. These are expenses of the year and bear no khums, and they never become liable later — not even if you stop needing them, and not the jewellery a woman has stopped wearing.
      • Things kept for a season. Winter clothes in summer are kept by their nature for another year, and are not counted as unused surplus.
      • Capital. The money in a business, its stock and its equipment are liable in full at the year's end, however necessary they are to the trade. Where paying at once would leave a person unable to earn a living befitting his standing, the case is put to the marjaʻ's office and terms are arranged; it is not for the payer to decide his own relief.
      • Money lent out. Liable when it can be recovered. What cannot presently be got back waits for the year it returns.

      What carries no khums at all

      Mahr received by a wife; what a husband receives in a khulʻ divorce; blood money; an inheritance received by the recognised laws of inheritance; and khums or zakat given to you. Note what is not on that list: a gift. Wealth that comes to you without earning it — a gift, a prize, a bequest — is liable on whatever of it remains beyond the year's expenses. And whatever any exempt wealth earns is income like any other.

      Two inheritances are liable all the same: one the deceased had not paid khums on — the heir pays it — and one that comes from a relation you had no expectation of inheriting from, which is a gain and not an inheritance in the ordinary sense.

      Debts

      • A debt incurred for the necessities of the household comes off the year's profit, whether or not it has yet been repaid.
      • A debt taken to enlarge your wealth, or to buy what you did not need, does not — unless the thing bought with it, or the money itself, is lost.
      • A loan taken to buy the home you live in is met by setting each year's surplus against the outstanding balance, until the amount so set aside over the years comes to the size of the loan. After that the repayments of capital are no longer deducted, though the interest is still an expense of the year.

      The two halves

      The fifth is divided in two.

      • The share of the Imam (peace be upon him) is given today to the fully qualified jurist — by obligatory precaution the most learned, and one aware of public affairs — or to whoever he authorises, or spent on what he permits. It may not be spent at one's own discretion.
      • The share of the sayyids goes to a sayyid — by descent through the father — who is poor, or an orphan, or stranded on a journey. A stranded sayyid may receive it even if he is not poor at home. It may not be given to someone whose maintenance is already your obligation, nor where it will be spent in sin. Routing it through the marjaʻ's office or his representative is the safe course, and is what the offices ask.

      By obligatory precaution, nothing but money may be handed over in place of khums except with the authorisation of a fully qualified jurist or his agent.

      If you have never paid

      Past years cannot be estimated into existence. Fix your khums day, reckon this year properly, and put the past to the marjaʻ or his representative, who will settle it (musalaha) on terms you can meet. Wealth on which khums is owed is not fully yours to dispose of until it is settled — which is the reason for doing it, and not a reason to delay.

      The rulings summarised here follow the Islamic Laws of Sayyid Ali al-Husayni al-Sistani and his office's published answers. This is a tool for arithmetic and for the ordinary cases; it is not a substitute for a qualified scholar, and it does not know your circumstances. A year close to the line, a business whose capital cannot be touched, an uncertain inheritance, a first reckoning after many years — take these to sistani.org or to your local representative.
